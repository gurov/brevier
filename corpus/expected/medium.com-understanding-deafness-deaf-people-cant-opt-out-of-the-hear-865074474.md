Someone once asked me a question that sounded thoughtful on the surface:

*How do deaf people decide whether to separate themselves into the deaf community or assimilate into the hearing world?*

It was asked with genuine curiosity. No offense was intended and none was taken. But the question itself revealed a stubborn, underlying myth that refuses to die:

The idea that deaf people can *separate* themselves from the hearing world.

*We can’t.*

The hearing world is everywhere. It’s the default setting of society. You don’t opt out of it any more than you opt out of gravity.

But the concern is always there. Even in today’s day and age, it’s not uncommon for parents of deaf children to be advised to avoid anything related to the deaf community, especially sign language.

Why? Because there’s a misguided belief that it isolates a deaf child from mainstream society.

Okay, then. Let’s take a look at what happens when someone dives headfirst into the deaf community. I gladly volunteer myself as Exhibit A.

I’m deeply rooted in deaf culture. I’m deaf. My wife is deaf. We went to Gallaudet University (a.k.a. *Deaf Mecca*). We work in deaf-related fields. We show up at deaf events. If anyone could theoretically “live in a deaf bubble,” it would be us.

And yet…

I walk outside and chat with our hearing neighbors.

At work, I interact with hearing colleagues, parents, and professionals all day long.

Lunch at a restaurant? Hearing server. Hearing cashier.

Stop at a store? Hearing salesperson.

Evening activities and neighborhood community events? I’ve been the only deaf person in the room more times than I can count.

Every single day, I interact with hearing people. A lot more than most people realize. The number is off the charts. I couldn’t escape hearing people even if I wanted to.

And I’m considered someone who is *immersed* in the deaf community.

It’s time to put this *deaf people are isolated* myth to rest. So let’s tackle the issue that’s most commonly misunderstood right here and now:

*Being part of the deaf community is not any kind of withdrawal from, escape from, or rejection of the hearing world at large.*

It’s a **connection** that offers a powerful sense of belonging. A sense of belonging that contributes to your social-emotional well-being in a way that you become a better person around *everyone* you encounter, no matter if deaf or hearing.

We’ll get back to that sense of belonging in a minute. But before we do that, we need to address yet another one of the myths that stoke the fear of isolation:

*If deaf kids use sign language, they’ll be cut off from the world.*

That hasn’t been my experience.

ASL didn’t isolate me — it gave me access.

In fact, it was an ASL interpreter who helped me survive a hearing high school. This is not an exaggeration: I was on the cusp of failing out of school. A thoughtful administrator suggested that an ASL interpreter would bridge the communication gap between me, my hearing classmates, and my hearing teachers.

That’s exactly what happened. The ASL access offered a *bridge* to the hearing world. It didn’t isolate me. In fact, had no such service been provided, *that’s* when I would have been isolated. Kicked to the curb.

So the real question isn’t:

*Does ASL isolate?*

Instead, it’s:

*What actually allows a deaf person to fully participate?*

Because if you’re struggling to keep up with conversations, if you’re missing information and constantly guessing what’s going on, *that’s* isolation.

Meanwhile, when you’re in an environment where you have fluency, access, and clarity, that’s *connection.* Everyone deserves the kind of effortless connection where they can just relax and be themselves.

There’s more. There’s also a quieter fear lurking underneath all of this.

Some hearing parents worry:

*“Will I lose my child to the deaf community?”*

It’s an understandable fear. But again, it’s based on the false assumption that connection to one world requires rejection of another.

It doesn’t.

Deaf people don’t stop having hearing families. We don’t disappear into some hidden society. We live in both worlds every single day.

If anything, being part of a strong community makes people *more* connected, not less.

Take any tight-knit group — cultural groups, church communities, athletic organizations, theater clubs, and any hobby circles — and you’ll see they don’t isolate people from society. They give people grounding, identity, and confidence.

The same is true for the deaf community.

It gives us a place where communication is effortless, a place where we’re not “the only one in the room” and don’t have to work twice as hard just to keep up.

That doesn’t shrink our world. It expands it.

There’s no fork in the road where deaf people have to decide: *it’s either the deaf community or the hearing world. Pick one and chuck the other out the window.* That choice doesn’t exist.

*(A side note: I’m pretty sure that most of my deaf friends who are reading this are screaming for me to let you know that the same thing applies to hearing people. Hearing people can and do learn ASL, and they are welcome in the deaf community. There’s no fork in their road, either.)*

What *does* exist is something far more powerful:

A sense of belonging, a sense of purpose, and the kind of solid footing that comes when you know you can thrive exactly as who you are.

Long story short: Instead of looking at this through an either/or lens… instead of looking at as a *choice*… look at it as an *opportunity.* A legit opportunity to have the best of both worlds.
