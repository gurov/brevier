Brevier opens a web page, reduces it to its text and structure — headings, paragraphs, lists, quotes, code, tables, images — and sets it in its own typography: one typeface, a comfortable line length and leading, warm paper in light or dark. No JavaScript runs and no site styles apply. The type is the reader's, not the site's.

What it does

• Articles, blogs, documentation and long reads come out as clean text. The page's own contents, the feeds it offers and its navigation sit on a shelf at the side.
• Search from the address bar: words that are not an address go to DuckDuckGo Lite, and the results come back as a list of links.
• RSS, Atom and JSON feeds, including feed files and feed:// links. A feed of comments on one page reads as a thread.
• Documentation straight from GitHub and GitLab repositories: type gh:owner/repo.
• Tabs, history, bookmarks, find on page, zoom, saving an article as Markdown (with its images, as a zip).
• Pages read in the last week open from a copy on the phone, with no download.
• Back and forward return to the place you were reading.
• It can be your default browser: links from other apps open in new tabs, and what needs JavaScript or a login goes on to your other browser in one tap.
• Check this page: a score from 0 to 100 for how well a page reads without JavaScript, with the reasons.

What it does not do

• Run JavaScript, apply site styles, keep cookies or log in.
• Show ads, count its users or ask for an account. Brevier reports nothing to anyone: it fetches the pages you open and what it takes to read them, and your searches go to DuckDuckGo.

Sites assembled entirely by JavaScript do not open. Brevier says so plainly and offers your other browser.

Images are decoded by Brevier's own memory-safe decoders, not the system's. Certificates are checked by Android, and a site Android does not trust does not open.

Brevier is free software, under MIT or Apache-2.0. The same core runs a GTK window on Linux.
